GitLab Premium vs. Ultimate – Feature Comparison
Our current GitLab Premium gives us robust CI/CD and collaboration tools, but GitLab Ultimate includes everything in Premium plus a host of advanced capabilities. Key additions in Ultimate include an entire suite of built-in security scanning (Static & Dynamic Application Security Testing, secret detection, dependency and container scanning, etc.), along with vulnerability management and security dashboards. Ultimate also unlocks high-level planning and management features that Premium lacks – for example, multi-level Epics (to organize work hierarchically), portfolio roadmaps, enterprise agile planning, and value stream management for end-to-end visibility. Another unique benefit of Ultimate is unlimited free Guest users on self-managed instances (more on the Guest role below). In short, Ultimate is the “all features included” edition – it has every feature Premium offers, and then adds on powerful security, compliance, and program management tools that can benefit our organization.

Leveraging “Guest” User Roles (Permissions & Billing)
A big part of our cost strategy is to demote certain users to the Guest role under Ultimate. In GitLab Free or Premium, even Guest users still count toward your license seat count, but on Ultimate, users with the Guest role do not consume a license seat at all ￼. This means we can have an unlimited number of Guest users on Ultimate without paying for them, as long as those users only have Guest permissions (and no higher access in any project/group). The trade-off is that Guest permissions are quite limited by design. A Guest can view project information (like issues, epics, high-level analytics, incident reports, etc.) and can perform basic contributions such as creating new issues or other work items. However, Guests cannot modify code or settings – in fact, by default a Guest cannot even view private repository code in GitLab. They essentially have read-only access to issues and some project metadata. (Ultimate does allow an admin to expand Guest permissions slightly – for example, you could create a custom role that grants “Guest” users read access to the repository code without counting as a full seat. But if you give a Guest any higher level capabilities beyond that, they would then count as a billable user.) Using this Guest role strategically, we plan to give our occasional or non-development users access to the projects without needing paid seats. They’ll be able to follow progress, view issues, and contribute to discussions, but won’t have write access to the code. This Guest user approach on Ultimate lets us include many stakeholders for free while controlling license costs ￼.

Seat Count and Pricing – From 40 Premium to 20 Ultimate
Currently we have 40 users on GitLab Premium. Premium is priced at $29 per user/month (billed annually), so roughly we’re paying on the order of $13k–$14k per year for those 40 seats. GitLab Ultimate is a higher tier (list price about $99 per user/month, i.e. $1188 per user/year). The idea is to switch to ~20 Ultimate seats for our active contributors and make the remaining users Guests. At list prices, 20 Ultimate seats would cost about the same (20 × $99/month ≈ $1,980/month, which annually is roughly $23.8k – we’ll need to verify exact quotes, but presumably we aim to negotiate this near our current spend). With Ultimate’s free guest user policy, those ~20 people we demote to Guest will no longer require a paid seat, yet they can still log in and access the projects in a limited capacity. Essentially, we’d be paying for 20 full users on Ultimate and get unlimited read-only (Guest) users for no extra cost. This keeps our effective cost about the same as the 40 Premium seats, while giving our core team the benefits of Ultimate. (It’s worth noting that if any “Guest” user later needs to do more (write code, etc.), we’d have to elevate their role and then they’d start consuming a license seat. But we have the flexibility to manage seats dynamically. Also, GitLab’s license model for self-managed is based on true-ups for the maximum concurrent users in a year – as long as we keep the count of active (non-Guest) users at or below 20, we won’t exceed our subscription. We can always increase the count later if needed.) For reference, here’s GitLab’s official pricing page which lists the features of Premium vs Ultimate (and mentions the free guest users on Ultimate). We should review our contract or talk to GitLab sales to ensure the pricing aligns, but in principle the plan is cost-neutral: fewer paid seats at a higher tier in exchange for more features.

Upgrading from GitLab 13 to Latest – What We Gain
Since we’re currently on GitLab 13.x, we are missing out on quite a few improvements that have been added in the last few years of releases. By upgrading to the current GitLab 16.x/17.x (the latest stable is in the 17 series as of early 2025), we’ll gain a ton of new features and bug fixes. Just to highlight a few:
	•	GitLab 15.0 (2022) – Introduced a WYSIWYG editor for Wikis and made container scanning available in all tiers ￼ (previously some security scans were Ultimate-only, they’ve since moved some down to lower tiers). It also added internal notes on issues/epics and other usability improvements. There were also breaking changes (removal of some deprecated 13.x features) that cleaned up the platform and improved performance.
	•	GitLab 16.0 (May 2023) – A major release with over 50 improvements. It launched the Value Streams Dashboard (for end-to-end DevOps metrics and DORA benchmarks), introduced Remote Development Workspaces, and improved the built-in AI-powered Code Suggestions feature. This was also around when the first GitLab Duo AI features were introduced (more on Duo below). Additionally, 16.0 brought more powerful SaaS runners and many pipeline enhancements.
	•	GitLab 16.1 – Brought an all-new navigation UI for GitLab, which modernized the look and feel and made the app easier to navigate ￼. This UI update is a big UX improvement over what we have in 13.x. 16.1 also made GitLab Dedicated generally available (a managed offering) and added features like Kubernetes resource visualization ￼.
	•	Ongoing 16.x Updates – Throughout the 16 series, there were monthly releases adding things like customizable roles (16.4 introduced custom roles which we can use to fine-tune Guest access, etc.), improved security features (e.g. centralized policy management, expanded compliance reports), and performance boosts. By 16.11, the GitLab Duo Chat AI became generally available (allowing AI assistance via chat directly in GitLab’s UI) – a big step forward in AI integration (see below).
	•	GitLab 17.x (2024–2025) – The improvements have continued. For example, GitLab 17.8 (Jan 2025) added enhanced security for container registries, new ML experiment tracking features, and the ability to list deployments related to a given release, among other updates ￼. The 17.x line has also included numerous bug fixes and quality-of-life improvements. (GitLab does monthly patch releases – since 13, hundreds of bug fixes have been delivered, so upgrading will resolve many known issues we might have encountered in the old version.)

Overall, moving from version 13 to the current version will give us a much richer and smoother GitLab experience. We’ll get the benefit of three+ years of new features, UI/UX enhancements, performance optimizations, and security fixes. This includes things like easier navigation, built-in security scanning (which in 13 might have been rudimentary), better planning tools, and integrated AI assistance. It’s a significant leap that should address some pain points we have with our older instance and open up new possibilities (like leveraging built-in security and analytics features that we simply didn’t have before). We will of course want to read the upgrade notes to handle any breaking changes between 13 and 17, but the value we gain is huge.

GitLab Duo (AI Assistant) – Status and Capabilities
One of the exciting additions in recent GitLab is GitLab Duo, the AI-powered assistant that GitLab has been rolling out. As of GitLab 16.11 (late 2024), GitLab Duo Chat is generally available and mature. This means we have access to a conversational AI assistant within GitLab that can help with a variety of development tasks. For example, Duo can explain code to you (great for understanding unfamiliar codebases), suggest code refactoring improvements, and even generate unit tests for a given code snippet. These features are accessible right in the GitLab web interface (there’s a chat panel and it’s integrated into the Web IDE) and also through IDE extensions (so you can use GitLab Duo in VS Code or JetBrains IDEs). In practice, this could speed up code reviews, help onboard new team members (by answering “how do I do X in GitLab?” questions in natural language), and automate some of the grunt work in writing code and tests.

It’s worth noting that GitLab Duo is offered as an add-on service on top of Ultimate/Premium. GitLab has a Duo Pro tier (about $19/user/month) which provides the core AI features like code completion/suggestions and the chat assistant. They also mention a Duo Enterprise tier (for even more advanced AI capabilities like summarization of merge requests, advanced vulnerability analysis, and self-hosted AI models) for Ultimate customers. For now, we would likely start with the basic Duo features (possibly trial the Pro add-on). The current status is that Duo’s AI features are fairly robust – Code Suggestions (the “AI pair programmer” feature similar to GitHub Copilot) has been improving since GitLab 16.0, and the Duo Chat interface brings a lot of those capabilities together in a user-friendly way. This could be a big productivity boost for us if we enable it. We should definitely take advantage of any trials to see how it works with our code. (Since we’ll be on Ultimate, we have the option to add this; it’s available to Premium and Ultimate subscribers as an add-on.)

Advanced Search (Elasticsearch Integration)
GitLab Ultimate (and Premium) include an Advanced Search feature powered by Elasticsearch/OpenSearch, which we’ve discussed but are considering for later. This is basically an integration that allows GitLab to index repository content and other data into an Elasticsearch cluster to enable fast, comprehensive searching. If we enable Advanced Search on our self-managed instance, we’d have to deploy an Elasticsearch (or OpenSearch) service and connect GitLab to it. Once set up, it provides faster search response times and richer search capabilities than the basic search. For example, advanced search lets you query across all projects for code snippets or text, search issues and merge requests by content, and even find things like file names or specific code patterns across the entire GitLab instance ￼. This can be extremely useful in a large codebase/organization – you can identify duplicated code or patterns, locate references to a library across all repos, search all issues for specific keywords, etc. GitLab’s documentation notes use cases like finding security vulnerabilities in dependencies globally, tracking usage of deprecated functions across projects, and generally discovering things that a single-project search would miss ￼. In short, it makes GitLab a powerful enterprise search tool for our code and project data.

At the moment, we’re interested in this Elasticsearch integration but may not adopt it immediately. The main reason is the overhead – we’d need to run and maintain a separate Elasticsearch cluster, and index all our data, which is a project in itself. It’s an option to keep in mind for the future. The nice thing is that once we’re on Ultimate, we have the ability to turn this on at any time (it’s included in the license). So perhaps after the initial upgrade, we can evaluate if our search needs (or the size of our codebase) justify setting up Elasticsearch. It’s good to know that this feature is there, and if we start feeling the limitations of the default GitLab search, we can plan to deploy the Advanced Search. I’ve included the link to GitLab’s docs on the Elasticsearch integration for reference, which also explains how to set it up. For now, we’ll likely hold off until we’re ready, but it’s on our radar as a potential enhancement down the road.

Conclusion – Why GitLab Ultimate and Next Steps
To summarize, moving to GitLab Ultimate will give us: (a) the full range of GitLab features (especially around security and planning) that we don’t have on Premium, (b) the ability to include many more users as Guests without additional cost (important for broad collaboration) ￼, (c) access to new capabilities like built-in AI assistance (GitLab Duo) and better search/analytics, and (d) all the improvements from the last several versions of GitLab – which means a faster, more secure, and more user-friendly platform than our old version ￼. By adjusting our user roles (making low-activity users Guests), we can achieve this upgrade in functionality while keeping costs roughly the same as our current Premium plan.

At this point, it looks like GitLab Ultimate is a great fit for our needs. We’ve practically finalized the decision; this information is mainly to ensure we’ve not missed anything and to communicate the benefits clearly. If you’re on board with this plan (and it sounds like you are), the next steps will be to coordinate the license upgrade and schedule our GitLab instance upgrade from 13 to the latest version. We should also prepare a communication to the team about the new features (and any changes in usage, like the new navigation or how Guest access works). Let’s double-check our seat counts and then pull the trigger on upgrading to Ultimate. It’s an exciting step – we’ll get a much more powerful GitLab setup for our team!
